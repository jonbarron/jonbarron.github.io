A Fix for Softmax and Cross‑Entropy

Jonathan T. Barronbarron@google.com
Dan Gnanapragasamnanabyte@google.com
Michael Tschannentschannen@google.com
Google DeepMind
arXiv:2610.31642
Abstract

Standard implementations of softmax, log-softmax, and cross-entropy have two numerical bugs. The first bug, Softmax Collapse, rounds the target logit’s gradient to zero if that one logit is much larger than the others, resulting in gradients that no longer sum to zero. The second bug, Softmax Cancellation, occurs when student logits are close to teacher logits, and produces non-zero gradients even when the student and teacher logits are equal. We present simple implementations of log-softmax, softmax, and cross-entropy that fix both bugs. Our fix eliminates the previously observed “slingshot” phenomenon, speeds up grokking with weight decay, prevents accuracy collapse without weight decay, stops student networks from drifting away from the teacher during distillation, and shows that after memorization, logit growth is bounded by an equilibrium between weight decay and Adam’s ϵ\epsilonϵ hyperparameter.

The first bug, identified by Prieto et al. When training with adaptive optimizers such as Adam and AdamW (Kingma & Ba, 2015; Loshchilov & Hutter, 2019), which normalize each gradient by a moving average of its magnitude, this asymmetry causes the recurring “slingshot” loss spikes (Thilak et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2026) and stalls grokking (Prieto et al., 2025). While prior works used surrogate losses (Prieto et al., 2025), gradient projection (Liu et al., 2026), or fp64 casting (Nanda et al., 2023; Varma et al., 2023) to ameliorate these issues, we instead fix log-softmax, softmax, and cross-entropy directly (Section 2).

The second bug, which we will call Softmax Cancellation (Figure 1b), occurs in knowledge distillation (Hinton et al., 2015) and KL regularization, where the target p=softmax⁡(zT)\mathbf{p}=\operatorname{softmax}(\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{T}})p=softmax(zT​) comes from teacher logits zT\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{T}}zT​. When zS\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{S}}zS​ is close to zT\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{T}}zT​, subtracting these nearly equal probabilities loses precision, and slight differences between the forward-pass softmax and backward-pass log-softmax formulas leave a non-zero gradient even when zS=zT\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{S}}=\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{T}}zS​=zT​. We fix this by computing q−p\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{p}q−p directly from zS−zT\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{S}}-\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{T}}zS​−zT​ in log space (Section 3).

Here is a JAX implementation of log-softmax, softmax, and hard-target cross-entropy, which evaluates log⁡(1+e−m)\log(1+e^{-m})log(1+e−m) via log1p⁡\operatorname{log1p}log1p, remains accurate down to exponent underflow, and satisfies ∑kgk=0\sum_{k}g_{k}=0∑k​gk​=0:

import jax, jax.numpy as jnp, optax

def log_softmax(z):
  i_max = jnp.argmax(z, axis=-1)
  z_max = jnp.take_along_axis(z, i_max[..., None], axis=-1)  # Max logit.
  is_max = jax.nn.one_hot(i_max, z.shape[-1], dtype=bool)    # Max mask.
  shifted = jnp.where(is_max, 0.0, z - z_max)                # Exact 0 at argmax.
  exp_others = jnp.where(is_max, 0.0, jnp.exp(shifted))      # Non-max exp.
  s = jnp.sum(exp_others, axis=-1, keepdims=True)            # Sum non-max exp.
  return shifted - jnp.log1p(s)                              # log_softmax(z).

softmax = lambda z: jnp.exp(log_softmax(z))

def cross_entropy(z, t):
  z_t = jnp.take_along_axis(z, t[..., None], axis=-1)        # Target logit.
  is_t = jax.nn.one_hot(t, z.shape[-1], dtype=bool)          # Target mask.
  shifted = jnp.where(is_t, 0.0, z - z_t)                    # Exact 0 at target.
  b = jnp.max(shifted, axis=-1, keepdims=True)               # Max shift >= 0.
  s = jnp.sum(jnp.where(is_t, 0.0, jnp.exp(shifted - b)), axis=-1)
  return b[..., 0] + jnp.log1p(s + jnp.expm1(-b[..., 0]))    # -log_softmax(z)_t.

3. Softmax Cancellation#

Given student logits zS∈RK\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{S}}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}zS​∈RK and teacher logits zT∈RK\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}zT​∈RK, standard soft-target cross-entropy computes p=softmax⁡(zT)\mathbf{p}=\operatorname{softmax}(\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{T}})p=softmax(zT​) and evaluates

L(zS,zT)=−∑kpk logsoftmax⁡(zS)k ,q=softmax⁡(zS) ,g~k=∂L∂zS,k=qk−pk .\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{S}},\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{T}})=-\sum_{k}p_{k}\,\operatorname{logsoftmax}(\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{S}})_{k}\,,\quad\mathbf{q}=\operatorname{softmax}(\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{S}})\,,\quad\tilde{g}_{k}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial z_{\mathrm{S},k}}=q_{k}-p_{k}\,.L(zS​,zT​)=−k∑​pk​logsoftmax(zS​)k​,q=softmax(zS​),g~​k​=∂zS,k​∂L​=qk​−pk​.

As the student logits approach the teacher logits (zS→zT\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{S}}\to\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{T}}zS​→zT​), the true gradient g=q−p\mathbf{g}=\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{p}g=q−p shrinks toward 0\mathbf{0}0 and equals 0\mathbf{0}0 when zS=zT\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{S}}=\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{T}}zS​=zT​. In floating point, however, computing q\mathbf{q}q and p\mathbf{p}p separately and subtracting them (Equation 8) loses precision when zS\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{S}}zS​ is close to zT\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{T}}zT​, because the small logit difference δ=zS−zT\boldsymbol{\delta}=\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{S}}-\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{T}}δ=zS​−zT​ is lost when q\mathbf{q}q and p\mathbf{p}p are rounded to probabilities before being subtracted (Figure 1b). Furthermore, because p=softmax⁡(zT)\mathbf{p}=\operatorname{softmax}(\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{T}})p=softmax(zT​) in the forward pass and q=exp⁡(logsoftmax⁡(zS))\mathbf{q}=\exp(\operatorname{logsoftmax}(\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{S}}))q=exp(logsoftmax(zS​)) in the backward pass are evaluated using slightly different sequences of operations, q\mathbf{q}q and p\mathbf{p}p do not match to machine precision even when zS=zT\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{S}}=\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{T}}zS​=zT​, leaving a non-zero gradient g~=q−p≠0\tilde{\mathbf{g}}=\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{p}\neq\mathbf{0}g~​=q−p=0.

To avoid subtracting nearly equal probabilities, we pass zT\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{T}}zT​ directly to our loss function and compute q−p\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{p}q−p in log space using expm1⁡(x)=ex−1\operatorname{expm1}(x)=e^{x}-1expm1(x)=ex−1 and log1p⁡(x)=log⁡(1+x)\operatorname{log1p}(x)=\log(1+x)log1p(x)=log(1+x). Because Equation 9 avoids adding or subtracting 111, g\mathbf{g}g remains accurate as zS→zT\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{S}}\to\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{T}}zS​→zT​ and is 0\mathbf{0}0 when zS=zT\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{S}}=\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{T}}zS​=zT​. Here is a JAX implementation of soft-target cross-entropy (using log_softmax and softmax from Section 2), which uses jax.lax.stop_gradient (sg) to return the forward loss while substituting g\mathbf{g}g in the backward pass:

def soft_target_cross_entropy(z_S, z_T):
  log_q = log_softmax(z_S)
  q, p = jnp.exp(log_q), softmax(z_T)
  delta = z_S - z_T                                                    # Logit difference.
  d = delta - jnp.sum(p * delta, axis=-1, keepdims=True)               # Center under p.
  s = d - jnp.log1p(jnp.sum(p * jnp.expm1(d), axis=-1, keepdims=True)) # log(q / p).
  u = jnp.where(jnp.abs(s) < 1, p * jnp.expm1(s), q - p)               # Difference q - p.
  g = u - q * jnp.sum(u, axis=-1, keepdims=True)                       # Ensure sum(g) = 0.
  sg = jax.lax.stop_gradient
  loss = -jnp.sum(p * sg(log_q), axis=-1)                              # Forward loss.
  return loss + jnp.sum(sg(g) * (z_S - sg(z_S)), axis=-1)              # Custom gradient g.

4. The Slingshot Mechanism#

In grokking (Power et al., 2022), a network quickly memorizes the training set (qt→1q_{t}\to 1qt​→1) and generalizes much later. While studying grokking on modular addition with Adam (Kingma & Ba, 2015; Loshchilov & Hutter, 2019), Thilak et al. (2022) observed the slingshot mechanism, where training after memorization alternates between flat loss plateaus near zero and sudden loss spikes that coincide with jumps in test accuracy.

These slingshot spikes are a direct consequence of Softmax Collapse (Section 2, Prieto et al. (2025); Liu et al. (2026)). Recall that AdamW updates parameters θ\boldsymbol{\theta}θ by subtracting η(m^/(v^+ϵ)+λθ)\eta\big{(}\hat{\mathbf{m}}/(\sqrt{\hat{\mathbf{v}}}+\epsilon)+\lambda\boldsymbol{\theta}\big{)}η(m^/(v^​+ϵ)+λθ), where m^\hat{\mathbf{m}}m^ and v^\hat{\mathbf{v}}v^ are bias-corrected moving averages of the parameter gradient g=∇θL(θ)\mathbf{g}=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})g=∇θ​L(θ) and its square (with decay rates β1,β2\beta_{1},\beta_{2}β1​,β2​). After memorization, the logit margins mmm grow as the network pushes its target probabilities toward 111. Once an example’s margin exceeds the precision cliff cdc_{d}cd​, standard cross-entropy rounds its target gradient g~t\tilde{g}_{t}g~​t​ to zero while its non-target gradients g~j>0\tilde{g}_{j}>0g~​j​>0 (j≠tj\neq tj=t) remain positive. When all examples in a batch sit above cdc_{d}cd​, the parameter gradients nearly vanish and Adam’s second moment v^i\hat{v}_{i}v^i​ decays exponentially toward zero while weight decay slowly shrinks the parameters. Eventually, weight decay or the residual non-target updates pull a few examples back below cdc_{d}cd​, where their target gradients g~t≈−e−m\tilde{g}_{t}\approx-e^{-m}g~​t​≈−e−m abruptly turn back on. Because v^i\sqrt{\hat{v}_{i}}v^i​​ decayed while those examples were above cdc_{d}cd​, Adam divides the restored gradients by a very small denominator v^i+ϵ\sqrt{\hat{v}_{i}}+\epsilonv^i​​+ϵ, producing a large parameter step that spikes the training loss, resets v^i\hat{v}_{i}v^i​, and repeats the cycle. With our fixed cross-entropy, the target-logit gradient gt=−e−m/(1+e−m)g_{t}=-e^{-m}/(1+e^{-m})gt​=−e−m/(1+e−m) does not round to zero at cdc_{d}cd​, so m^i\hat{m}_{i}m^i​ and v^i\sqrt{\hat{v}_{i}}v^i​​ decay smoothly together and slingshot spikes disappear (Figure 2).

Whether slingshot spikes appear to help or hurt generalization depends on how the network’s weight norm is controlled (Figure 2). When a network uses LayerNorm before the output layer and no weight decay (Thilak et al., 2022; Prieto et al., 2025), growing the pre-LayerNorm weights does not change the logits, but it does shrink the effective step size η/∥θ∥2\eta/\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|_{2}η/∥θ∥2​; each slingshot spike inflates ∥θ∥2\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|_{2}∥θ∥2​ and acts as an accidental learning-rate decay. When weight norms are already controlled by weight decay (Varma et al., 2023), slingshot spikes at c32c_{32}c32​ only slow down and hurt grokking (94±5%94\pm 5\%94±5% vs. 100±0%100\pm 0\%100±0% test accuracy on a+b mod 31a+b\bmod 31a+bmod31; Figure 2, top). And when neither LayerNorm nor weight decay is used (Doshi et al., 2024), a single slingshot spike at c32c_{32}c32​ is destructive: on a2+b2 mod 97a^{2}+b^{2}\bmod 97a2+b2mod97 (λ=0\lambda=0λ=0, ϵ=10−16\epsilon=10^{-16}ϵ=10−16), standard cross-entropy tracks fixed cross-entropy up to 61%61\%61% test accuracy until mmm hits c32c_{32}c32​ around step 125012501250, where a spike drops test accuracy to 23%23\%23% (and from 60%60\%60% to 21%21\%21% on a3+b mod 97a^{3}+b\bmod 97a3+bmod97), while fixed cross-entropy continues smoothly to 85%85\%85% (Figure 2, bottom).

7. Soft-Target Distillation#

To see how Softmax Cancellation (Section 3) affects optimization, we initialize a 2-layer ReLU MLP student with the fp32 weights of a teacher trained to 100%100\%100% accuracy on a+b mod 53a+b\bmod 53a+bmod53 (θS(0)=θT\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{S}}^{(0)}=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{T}}θS(0)​=θT​), and run 2,0002{,}0002,000 full-batch steps of Adam (η=2×10−3\eta=2\times 10^{-3}η=2×10−3, β1=0.9\beta_{1}=0.9β1​=0.9, β2=0.98\beta_{2}=0.98β2​=0.98, λ=0\lambda=0λ=0, ϵ=10−8\epsilon=10^{-8}ϵ=10−8) on the 50%50\%50% training split at temperature T=4T=4T=4 (Hinton et al., 2015) (which keeps m/T<c16m/T<c_{16}m/T<c16​ so p\mathbf{p}p does not round to one-hot), computing the forward pass and loss in bf16, fp32, or fp64. Because zS=zT\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{S}}=\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{T}}zS​=zT​ initially, the true gradient is zero and the student should not move. However, standard soft-target cross-entropy has a non-zero residual gradient at zS=zT\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{S}}=\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{T}}zS​=zT​ due to the mismatch between its forward softmax and backward log-softmax (Figure 1b). Because Adam divides by v^+ϵ\sqrt{\hat{\mathbf{v}}}+\epsilonv^​+ϵ, it amplifies even tiny residual gradients (Figure 7): in bf16 (∼10−3{\sim}10^{-3}∼10−3) it drives the student away from the teacher and degrades test accuracy, in fp32 (∼10−8{\sim}10^{-8}∼10−8) it causes an initial KL\mathrm{KL}KL spike and a ∼10−6{\sim}10^{-6}∼10−6 floor, and in fp64 (∼10−17{\sim}10^{-17}∼10−17) the update is too small to change the fp32 weights. Our fixed soft-target cross-entropy returns g=0\mathbf{g}=\mathbf{0}g=0 at zS=zT\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{S}}=\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{T}}zS​=zT​, so the student stays at KL=0\mathrm{KL}=0KL=0 and 100%100\%100% accuracy across all precisions.

8. Conclusion#

We have presented simple fixes for two numerical bugs in standard implementations of softmax, log-softmax, and cross-entropy: Softmax Collapse, which zeroes out the dominant logit’s gradient at high confidence while leaving non-dominant gradients non-zero, and Softmax Cancellation, which loses precision when student logits approach teacher logits in soft-target distillation. Rewriting these functions in log space using softplus⁡\operatorname{softplus}softplus, expm1⁡\operatorname{expm1}expm1, and log1p⁡\operatorname{log1p}log1p restores accurate, zero-sum gradients across bf16, fp32, and fp64 without internally casting to higher precision. Fixing these bugs eliminates slingshot loss spikes during grokking, prevents student networks from drifting away from the teacher during distillation, and reveals that logit margins under AdamW and weight decay naturally converge to log⁡(1 ⁣/ ⁣ϵ)\log(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\epsilon}})log(1/ϵ​). Though the extreme margins and near-zero student–teacher gaps probed by our experiments are uncommon in standard large-scale training, our fixed functions are easy drop-in replacements that can be implemented without meaningful slowdown, and may prove useful whenever models make high-confidence predictions or distill from close teachers.
